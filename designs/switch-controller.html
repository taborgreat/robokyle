Design

Switch-Adapted Game Controller

A drop-in replacement shell for a standard console controller, with four 3.5mm mono jacks wired in parallel to the four face buttons. Any accessibility switch that ends in a 3.5mm plug — the same shape as a pair of wired headphones — can trigger any of the buttons, either on its own or alongside the original button.

It helps players with limited hand mobility, tremor, or one-handed play get on parity with anyone else at the couch, without paying $400 for a first-party adaptive controller.

What’s honest about the status

V2 shell has been printed by five people successfully. The wiring diagram works on Xbox Series and PS5 stock controllers; Switch Pro needs a different pinout that we haven’t documented yet.

  • PartsStock controller, 4 × 3.5mm PCB-mount jacks, 22 AWG wire, M3 heat-set inserts.
  • Tools3D printer (PETG or ABS), soldering iron, small Phillips driver.
  • Rough cost$8–$12 in parts on top of the controller you already own.
  • Build timeAbout 90 minutes plus print time (roughly 5 hours at 0.2mm).

How it works and why we designed it this way

Modern game controllers are dense little computers. Rather than replace the board, we solder short leads onto the underside of each face-button dome contact. Those leads run to four 3.5mm jacks mounted along the top edge of a custom printed shell.

When you plug a switch into a jack, it’s wired in parallel with the physical button — press either one and the controller sees the same signal. Nothing about the original controller’s firmware or Bluetooth pairing changes, which is why it works out of the box with every game.

Why 3.5mm jacks

Accessibility switches from Ablenet, Enabling Devices, and every DIY kit we could find land on 3.5mm mono. Standardising on that jack means anyone can bring their existing switches to your controller without adapters.

Why not Bluetooth?

Wireless switches add pairing failures, batteries, and latency. Wired keeps the total system cost under $15 and removes an entire category of things that can break mid-game.

What we got wrong, and what we would change

  • The V1 shell had 2mm walls at the jack mounts — they cracked after ~20 unplug cycles. V2 moves that to 3.5mm with a chamfer.
  • We didn’t label jack polarity on the shell. People plugged switches in backwards and thought the controller was broken. V3 will screen-print or emboss the label.
  • Solder pads on newer boards are smaller than we planned for. If we started again we’d design a small breakout PCB rather than direct-soldering the dome contacts.
