Build tutorial

Switch-Adapted Game Controller

End-to-end build of the switch-adapted controller. Follow it top to bottom — each step is one instruction, one photo. If any step doesn’t match what you’re seeing, open a GitHub issue and we’ll pair on it.

Estimated time
~90 minutes + print time
Skill level
Comfortable with a soldering iron
Cost
$8–$12 + controller

Parts

  • 1 × stock console controller (Xbox Series or PS5)
  • 4 × PCB-mount 3.5mm mono jacks (Kycon STX-3100 or equivalent)
  • ~30cm of 22 AWG stranded wire, ideally four colours
  • 4 × M3 × 5mm heat-set inserts
  • 4 × M3 × 8mm socket-head screws
  • 1 × printed shell (STL in the download pack)

Tools

  • 3D printer with PETG or ABS filament (~5 hours print at 0.2mm)
  • Soldering iron with a fine tip
  • Small Phillips screwdriver (PH0 or PH00)
  • Wire strippers
  • Flush cutters
  • Multimeter with continuity mode
  • Isopropyl alcohol and a lint-free cloth

  1. Print the shell

    Slice the STL at 0.2mm layer height, 4 walls, 30% infill. PETG is preferred for the jack mounts — PLA works but wears faster on repeat unplug cycles.

    Photo — printer with finished shell on bed

  2. Install the heat-set inserts

    Press one M3 heat-set insert into each of the four screw bosses on the shell’s underside. Use a soldering iron at 220°C, and go straight in — if the insert tilts, back off and reheat before pushing further.

    Photo — heat-set insert going into shell

  3. Open the stock controller

    Remove the battery cover and battery. Undo the five Phillips screws on the back plate. Lift the back plate off, then unclip the ribbon cable connecting the battery bay before setting it aside.

    Photo — controller opened, screws visible

  4. Identify the face-button pads

    On the back of the main board, locate the four solder pads for the face buttons (A, B, X, Y or their PS equivalents). Test with a multimeter in continuity mode: touch one probe to the pad, the other to a ground point, then press the button. Continuity confirms the pad.

    Photo — multimeter probing button pad

  5. Solder leads to each button pad

    Cut four 8cm lengths of wire in four colours. Strip 2mm from one end of each and tin the tip. Solder one wire to each face-button pad; keep the joint low and flat so it clears the back plate.

    Photo — wires soldered to pads

  6. Solder a common ground

    Cut a fifth wire the same length. Solder it to any labelled GND pad on the board. This will feed into the sleeve terminal of every jack.

    Photo — ground wire soldered to GND pad

  7. Wire the jacks

    For each 3.5mm jack: solder the corresponding button wire to the tip terminal, and a short bridge from the ground wire to the sleeve terminal. Repeat for all four jacks. Double-check with continuity mode before closing anything.

    Photo — jack with wires soldered

  8. Seat the board in the printed shell

    Route the four jacks through the printed slots in the top edge of the shell. Confirm each jack sits flush with the outside surface and the retaining nut is snug on the inside.

    Photo — board in shell with jacks mounted

  9. Close the case and screw it down

    Reconnect the battery ribbon. Lower the back plate on. Drive the four M3 screws into the heat-set inserts — snug, not tight. Reinstall the battery and the battery cover.

    Photo — screws being driven into shell

  10. Test each jack

    Power on the controller and pair it as normal. Plug a switch into each jack in turn and confirm it fires the correct button in the console’s controller-test screen. If a jack is silent, check continuity on that jack’s ground bridge before re-opening the case.

    Photo — switch plugged into finished controller

  11. Wipe it down and hand it off

    Isopropyl on a lint-free cloth cleans up flux residue and fingerprints. You’re done — the controller works exactly like a stock one, with four new switch inputs alongside the original buttons.

    Photo — finished controller in use
